With ‘AI Slop’ Distorting Our Reality, The World is Sleepwalking Into Disaster


The rapid rise in popularity of AI content is damaging for media consumers, who are living in a constant frenzy of misinformation and fake content, and for artists, who are being replaced by AI programs. In my experience, as someone who usually does a few seconds of research before believing what I see online, it is becoming increasingly challenging to spot fake videos and news on social media. Not only are fake videos and images becoming of higher quality, but more people and platforms are creating and posting them. A few years ago, it would have been unthinkable that an institution like the White House would post fake news and AI content. However, we are seeing government officials not only share that type of content but also encourage its use. For me, seeing that we can't even trust government communications as reliable sources of information, generates a sense of confusion and anxiety over what's real versus what's propaganda. Moreover, as Malik points out, the "torrent of AI content leads to the desensitisation and overwhelming of visual palates." With social media apps like TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram overwhelming us with a never-ending stream of content, it has become impossible for users like me to remember most of the videos we see online. Real artists get lost in the sea of content, and both artists and consumers miss out on the opportunity to connect through real art.
Another concerning aspect of the popularity of AI content is that it is replacing and overpowering images, films, and art made by humans. "To social media giants, content is content; the cheaper it is, the less human labour it involves, the better." The more social media companies capitalize on this cheap content, the more real artists suffer. The "virality" of videos and images created with AI reduces job opportunities for individuals and results in a notable lack of creativity and diversity in the media we consume. The article warns that AI content is "inherently biased against ethnic diversity, progressive gender roles and sexual orientations, therefore [it concentrates] those norms in the output." The popularity of content that reflects those ideals generates more demand, which the AI machines can endlessly fulfill. That popularity becomes detrimental when it reaches vulnerable groups, such as the elderly, young children, and people with poor media literacy, who are unaware of the content's origin and naively trust its reliability. When those people then share the AI content they see through private (unfiltered and unchallenged) channels, like WhatsApp, the content spreads and starts gaining more and more "reliability." The article explains that the content, which is already high-quality enough to pass as real, gets "imbued with the authority and reliability of the person who has sent it." I would also add that the more people believe something to be real, the more reliable it seems to those seeing it for the first time. Moreover, in recent years, people's phones have become "echo chambers" that make it almost impossible for social media users to encounter content that challenges their preexisting beliefs. I believe that constant confirmation bias is already creating cracks in society that make it increasingly harder for people to overcome gender, political, and class barriers to have nuanced conversations.
